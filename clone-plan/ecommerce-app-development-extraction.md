### 2. Why most eCommerce apps get built and then abandoned
- Eyebrow/label: `The Problem`
- H2: `Why most eCommerce apps get built and then abandoned`
- Intro: `Twelve reasons app projects fail, and almost none of them is a development problem. We raise these before quoting, because the cheapest app project is the one you decide not to do.`
- 12 cards:
  1. `It duplicates the mobile site` — `An app that does exactly what your website already does on a phone gives a shopper no reason to install it, and no reason to keep it.`
  2. `Nobody owns installs` — `The build gets budget and the acquisition does not. An app with no install channel is a product with no distribution.`
  3. `Retention was never a target` — `Apps are judged on day-30 retention, not downloads. If nobody agreed a number before the build, nobody can tell afterwards whether it worked.`
  4. `The catalogue does not suit it` — `Apps reward frequent, habitual purchase. A catalogue bought once every two years will not generate the reopen rate an app needs to justify itself.`
  5. `Two codebases, two release trains` — `Separate native iOS and Android builds double the maintenance and guarantee the two drift apart. Most brands cannot resource that honestly.`
  6. `Checkout was rebuilt from scratch` — `A hand-built app checkout rarely matches the hosted one it replaced, and wallet support is where it usually falls short.`
  7. `No plan for store review` — `App Store and Play review can add a week to any release and can reject one outright. Teams that have not budgeted for it miss launch dates.`
  8. `Deep links were an afterthought` — `Without universal links and app indexing, every campaign, email and search result sends app users back to the website.`
  9. `Push became spam` — `Notification permission is granted once and revoked permanently. Overused in month one, the channel is gone by month three.`
  10. `Nobody budgeted for upkeep` — `Two operating systems ship breaking changes every year. An app without a maintenance retainer stops working, and the first sign is usually a one-star review.`
  11. `Analytics never matched the website` — `App and web analytics use different identity models. Without deliberate work the app looks either far better or far worse than it is.`
  12. `It was built on a template` — `App-builder templates launch quickly and hit a ceiling almost immediately, and the code is rarely portable when you outgrow it.`
- Quirk: British spelling `catalogue` in card 4 (kept verbatim).

### 12. eCommerce app development FAQs
- Eyebrow/label: `FAQs`
- H2: `eCommerce app development FAQs`
- Intro: `Eight questions that come up before most app projects. More on our full FAQ page .` (Quirk: space before the period; link text `our full FAQ page` → /faqs/ .)
- 8 FAQs (also in JSON-LD FAQPage, verbatim):
  1. Q: `Do we actually need an app?` A: `Probably not, and we would rather establish that before you spend anything. Apps earn their place when customers buy frequently and you have an owned channel capable of driving installs without paid spend. If purchases are occasional, or if the mobile site converts at half the desktop rate, the money goes further on fixing the mobile experience . We run that qualification first and we have talked brands out of app projects on the strength of it.` (Quirk: space before the period after `fixing the mobile experience`; link text `fixing the mobile experience` → /conversion-rate-optimization/ .)
  2. Q: `How long does eCommerce app development take?` A: `A private platform app scoped to one job is the quickest. A progressive web app is next, because it shares a codebase with the website. A cross-platform shopping app with wallet checkout, push, deep links and analytics is the longest, and the store review queue adds time at the end that is outside anyone’s control. The variable that moves app timelines most is not development speed — it is how quickly product data, content and decisions come back from your side, which we scope explicitly at planning.`
  3. Q: `What does an eCommerce app cost?` A: `It depends on which of the three build types fits and how much of the commerce layer already exists as a usable API. A private platform app is the smallest piece of work here; a progressive web app sits in the middle because it shares code with the website; a full cross-platform shopping app is several times either. We scope from requirements rather than quoting a package, and our pricing page explains the approach. Budget for the maintenance retainer as well as the build — an app without one stops working within about a year.` (Link text `our pricing page` → /pricing/ .)
  4. Q: `Native or cross-platform?` A: `Cross-platform, unless there is a specific reason not to be. One React Native codebase shipping to both stores halves the maintenance and keeps the two platforms from drifting apart, which they always do when they are separate projects. Go native when you need device capabilities React Native does not reach well, when performance in a specific interaction genuinely matters, or when you already employ native engineers. Two native codebases is a standing commitment most brands underestimate.`
  5. Q: `What about a progressive web app instead?` A: `Seriously consider it. A progressive web app gives you a home-screen icon, offline browsing, push notifications and no store review queue, from one codebase shared with your website, and nothing sold through it carries a store commission. The trade-offs are real: push on iOS is more limited, you get no app store listing as a discovery channel, and some device features are out of reach. For most brands who ask us for an app, this is the honest recommendation.`
  6. Q: `Who owns the code and the store listings?` A: `You do, throughout. The repository, the Apple and Google developer accounts, the signing keys and the store listings are all in your name from the first day rather than transferred at the end. We document the build and the release process, so if you take it in-house or move to another agency the app keeps shipping. There is no version of this where leaving us breaks your app, and we would not ask you to sign one.`
  7. Q: `How do we get people to install it?` A: `Installs are a marketing problem, not a build problem, and it is the half that usually goes unfunded. The channels that work are the ones you already own: email and SMS to existing customers, a prompt on the mobile site at the point of highest intent, packaging inserts, and post-purchase order tracking as the reason to install. Paid install campaigns are expensive and the installs are rarely worth what they cost. We scope the install plan alongside the build, because an app nobody installs is the most expensive thing on this page.`
  8. Q: `Can you take over an app somebody else built?` A: `Yes. The first step is an audit of the codebase and the analytics together, because the two questions are whether the app is technically salvageable and whether anybody is using it. Sometimes the honest answer is that a rebuild costs less than continuing to patch, and sometimes it is that the app should be retired and the budget moved to the mobile site. We will tell you which before you commit anything.`
